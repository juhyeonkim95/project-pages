4.2.2 Monte Carlo to the Rescue… Again

We wish to turn the boundary–integral formulation of the Poisson problem into a practical estimator. The boundary term is handled recursively by sampling a point on the largest sphere about the current position \(x_k\). For the source term, however, we draw samples uniformly inside the ball (disk) \(B(x_k)\) and form a Monte Carlo estimate. Throughout, the Green’s function \(G\) and source \(f\) are known, so we can evaluate the integrand directly without nested dependence on the unknown solution \(u\).

\[ \hat{S}(x_k) \;=\; \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} G(x_k, y_i)\,f(y_i), \qquad y_i \sim \mathcal{U}\!\big(B(x_k)\big) \tag{4.8} \]

Unlike the boundary contribution—where \(u\) appears inside the integrand—the source estimator uses only known quantities. Hence, variance can be reduced with standard tools: importance sampling, control variates, or stratification. For instance, if \(G(x_k,\cdot)\,f(\cdot)\) is radially concentrated, a radial importance density \(\propto r\,G(x_k,y)f(y)\) inside the disk can substantially reduce variance compared to the uniform law.

Writing the disk integral in polar coordinates \(y = x_k + r\,(\cos\phi,\sin\phi)\) with \(r\in[0,R]\), \(\phi\in[0,2\pi)\), we obtain

\[ \int_{B(x_k)} G(x_k,y)\,f(y)\,dy \;=\; \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{R} G\!\big(x_k, x_k + r\,\mathbf{e}_\phi\big)\, f\!\big(x_k + r\,\mathbf{e}_\phi\big)\, r\,dr\,d\phi, \tag{4.9} \]

A simple importance strategy chooses a separable density \(p(r,\phi)=p_r(r)\,p_\phi(\phi)\) with \(p_\phi(\phi)=\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\) and a radial law \(p_r(r)\propto r\,w(r)\). The corresponding estimator is

\[ \hat{S}_{\mathrm{IS}}(x_k) \;=\; \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M \frac{ G\big(x_k, x_k + r_i\,\mathbf{e}_{\phi_i}\big)\, f\big(x_k + r_i\,\mathbf{e}_{\phi_i}\big)\, r_i }{ p_r(r_i)\,p_\phi(\phi_i) }, \qquad r_i\sim p_r,\;\phi_i\sim p_\phi. \tag{4.10} \]

Remark. Since the walk will evaluate a source term at each step, a single sample (\(M=1\)) is often sufficient per step; variance is then controlled by the number of walk steps and by the importance distribution.

1.1 Problem Setup

We adopt the classical Poisson model \[ -\,\Delta u(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; f(\mathbf{x}) \quad \text{in } \Omega, \qquad u(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; g(\mathbf{x}) \quad \text{on } \partial\Omega, \tag{1.1} \] where \(f\) is a given source term and \(g\) specifies Dirichlet boundary data. For compactness we denote \(\langle a,b\rangle\) the Euclidean inner product.

1.1.1 Geometry and Variables

Let \(B(\mathbf{x};R)\) be a disk of radius \(R\) centered at \(\mathbf{x}\). We will uniformly sample points \(\mathbf{y}\in B(\mathbf{x};R)\) to estimate local contributions. We use the Green’s function \(G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\) for \(-\Delta\) in \(\Omega\).

\[ \mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \int_{B(\mathbf{x};R)} G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,f(\mathbf{y})\,d\mathbf{y}. \tag{1.2} \]

In polar coordinates \(\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{e}_\phi\), the integral becomes \[ \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{R} G\!\big(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{e}_\phi\big)\, f\!\big(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{e}_\phi\big)\, r\,dr\,d\phi. \tag{1.3} \]

1.1.2 Monte Carlo Estimator

With i.i.d. samples \(\mathbf{y}_i\sim \mathcal{U}(B(\mathbf{x};R))\), we estimate \(\mathcal{S}\) by \[ \widehat{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_i)\,f(\mathbf{y}_i), \qquad \mathbf{y}_i \sim \mathcal{U}\!\big(B(\mathbf{x};R)\big). \tag{1.4} \] Variance can be reduced by importance sampling with density \(p(\mathbf{y})\): \[ \widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{IS}}(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M \frac{ G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_i)\,f(\mathbf{y}_i) }{ p(\mathbf{y}_i) }, \qquad \mathbf{y}_i\sim p. \tag{1.5} \]

Remark 1.1. If \(G(\mathbf{x},\cdot)\,f(\cdot)\) is radially concentrated, use a separable \(p(r,\phi)=p_r(r)p_\phi(\phi)\) with \(p_\phi(\phi)=\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\) and \(p_r(r)\propto r\,w(r)\).

1.2 Practical Considerations

  • Sample count. A small \(M\) per step often suffices when many steps are taken.
  • Control variates. Subtract a cheap surrogate and add back its integral.
  • Stratification. Partition the disk by radius bands to reduce variance.

Section 2 — Algorithms

2.1 Random Walk with Source Term

We interleave boundary sampling with the source estimate from (1.4). Let \(\mathbf{x}_k\) be the current point and \(R_k\) the largest admissible radius. A single step uses:

  1. Draw \(\mathbf{y}\sim \mathcal{U}\!\big(B(\mathbf{x}_k; R_k)\big)\) and accumulate \(G(\mathbf{x}_k,\mathbf{y})f(\mathbf{y})\).
  2. Sample the next boundary point on \(\partial B(\mathbf{x}_k; R_k)\) and recurse.

2.1.1 Pseudocode

// RandomWalkWithSource(x0, steps, M)
accum ← 0
x ← x0
for k = 1..steps:
  R ← LargestAdmissibleRadius(x)
  for i = 1..M:
    y ← UniformDisk(x, R)
    accum ← accum + G(x, y) * f(y)
  x ← SampleOnCircle(x, R)  // boundary recursion
return accum / M

2.1.2 Cost and Variance

Per step cost is \(O(M)\). For fixed work \(W = M \times \text{steps}\), pick \(M\) to match the integrand’s variability; stratification in \(r\) can reduce the effective variance constant.
